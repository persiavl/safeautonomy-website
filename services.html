One hazard ecosystem

ISO 26262, ISO 21448 and ISO/PAS 8800 are not three competing rulebooks. They are three views of the same question — can this vehicle hurt someone, and how do we know? Here is what each one actually covers, where the boundaries sit, and why the boundaries are the dangerous part.

Start here

Each standard answers a different question

The fastest way to know which framework applies is to ask what has to go wrong for a person to be harmed. The three answers below are mutually exclusive as causes — and jointly exhaustive as an argument.

ISO 26262

"Does it fail dangerously?"

Something in the electrical/electronic system breaks or was built wrong — a random hardware fault, a systematic design fault, a software defect — and the resulting malfunctioning behaviour leads to a hazard.

The system is faulted. Remove the fault and the hazard disappears.

ISO 21448 — SOTIF

"Is it good enough when nothing fails?"

Nothing is broken. Every requirement is met. The function is simply not capable enough for the situation it meets — or a person uses it in a way that was foreseeable but not designed for.

The system is fault-free. You cannot debug your way out; you have to improve the function or restrict its operational domain.

ISO/PAS 8800

"Can the AI inside it be trusted?"

The insufficiency exists because a component was learned rather than written. Its behaviour comes from data, an optimisation process and an architecture — none of which can be reviewed clause by clause.

The system is fault-free and the function was well specified, but the evidence model from the other two standards does not fit.

Why it matters

Why three standards had to exist

Each one was written because the previous one had run out of road. The history is not trivia — it tells you exactly what each framework is blind to.

2011

ISO 26262 first edition — second edition 2018

2022

ISO 21448 published as a full standard

2024

ISO/PAS 8800 published for safety and AI

ISO 26262 assumed you could specify the right behaviour

Functional safety rests on a quiet assumption: that the correct behaviour of the item is known, and the only problem is making the electronics deliver it reliably. For an airbag controller or an electric power steering ECU, that assumption holds. The specification is short, the environment is bounded, and "correct" is unambiguous.

Automated driving broke the assumption. When the item has to decide whether a shape at 80 metres is a stopped vehicle or an overhead sign, the hard part is no longer delivering the specified behaviour — it is knowing what the specified behaviour should be. A perfectly fault-free implementation of an insufficient specification is still a hazard, and ISO 26262 has no clause for it.

ISO 21448 named the gap: insufficiency, not fault

SOTIF took the hazards that remain when nothing is broken and gave them a structure. Its central move is to stop reasoning about components and start reasoning about scenarios: the known-safe ones, the known-unsafe ones you must engineer away, and — the hard part — the unknown-unsafe ones you have not met yet.

The corresponding concept is the triggering condition: the specific circumstance that activates a latent insufficiency. Low sun at a particular elevation. A cyclist carrying a large flat object. Retro-reflective road works markings. None of these are faults. All of them are hazards.

The practical consequence. Under ISO 26262 you reduce risk by adding safety mechanisms. Under SOTIF you reduce risk by improving the function, restricting the operational design domain, or proving the residual unknown space is small enough to accept. These are entirely different engineering programmes with different budgets.

ISO/PAS 8800 addressed how the insufficiency gets in

SOTIF tells you that insufficiencies exist and must be bounded. It does not tell you how to develop, evaluate and argue about a component whose behaviour was learned. That is the gap ISO/PAS 8800 fills: an AI safety lifecycle that sits alongside the existing ones and produces evidence the other two standards can consume.

It covers the things that decide whether a machine-learned function is safe long before anyone runs a scenario test — how the dataset was specified and shown to cover the ODD, how training and evaluation were separated, how performance degrades at the edges of the distribution, how the system detects that it is out of distribution at runtime, and what architectural measures contain a wrong answer when one is produced anyway.

And then the law arrives

None of this is optional once you want to operate. Germany's autonomous driving act (StVG §1d–1l) and the implementing AFGBV ordinance allow SAE Level 4 vehicles to operate in a defined operational area — but demand a technical supervisor, a demonstrable risk-minimal state, data recording, independent technical assessment and a dossier the KBA will accept. At UNECE level, R157 and its successors do the same for type approval. The EU AI Act adds obligations on data governance, logging and human oversight for AI in safety components.

The regulator does not receive three standards. It receives one argument. If your evidence is organised by standard rather than by hazard, you will spend the last three months of the programme re-organising it under time pressure.

The ecosystem

How they combine into one hazard ecosystem

There is only ever one hazard list. The standards do not partition the vehicle — they partition the causes. That single idea is what makes an integrated safety argument possible.

Step 1  ·  One hazard analysis and risk assessment Item definition and ODD → hazardous events → safety goals. Performed once, jointly, not three times in three teams.
Branch A · ISO 26262

Fault-driven causes

Each safety goal gets an ASIL. Safety mechanisms, diagnostics and architectural measures reduce the probability that a fault produces the hazard.

Branch B · ISO 21448

Insufficiency-driven causes

The same safety goals are re-examined assuming zero faults. Triggering conditions, ODD limits and scenario coverage bound the performance risk.

Branch C · ISO/PAS 8800

AI-driven causes

Where a branch-B insufficiency lives inside a learned component, 8800 supplies the lifecycle and the evidence that branch B assumes exists.

Step 2  ·  One integrated safety case Every safety goal carries all three branches of evidence, with an explicit statement of what remains unproven.
Step 3  ·  Approval and operation AFGBV / StVG for L4 operation in Germany · UNECE type approval · ISO 21434 security case · EU AI Act obligations · field monitoring feeding back into step 1.

The seams

Three places where real programmes leak

In our experience the findings that hurt most are never inside one standard. They sit exactly on the boundary, where each team assumes the other one owns it.

Seam 01

26262  ↔  SOTIF

A safety mechanism is implemented to ASIL D and verified perfectly — against a requirement that was itself insufficient. Functional safety confirms the implementation; nobody confirms the intent.

Fix: derive SOTIF-relevant requirements from the same safety goals and trace them in the same requirement baseline, not in a parallel document set.

Seam 02

SOTIF  ↔  8800

A triggering condition is logged as an environmental edge case when it is really a dataset gap. The team responds with more road testing instead of a dataset change, and the same failure returns two releases later.

Fix: classify every triggering condition by whether its root cause is sensing physics, specification, or the training distribution — and route it accordingly.

Seam 03

8800  ↔  26262

The model is evaluated thoroughly, on hardware whose fault behaviour was never analysed for it. Accelerator memory faults, quantisation and non-deterministic scheduling all sit outside the ML evaluation and outside the classic FMEDA.

Fix: treat the inference platform as part of the item, with dependent failure analysis that includes the model's sensitivity to corrupted computation.

Cross-cutting

Work that spans all three

The integration itself is a deliverable. These engagements are what turn three conformant workstreams into one argument.

Scope an engagement
  • A
    Integrated safety case architecture. A goal-structured argument spanning functional safety, SOTIF and AI evidence, with explicit context and residual risk claims.
  • B
    Joint HARA & SOTIF hazard workshops. One hazard list, three causal branches, facilitated with your system, perception and validation teams in the room together.
  • C
    ODD and scenario strategy. A machine-readable operational design domain, a scenario catalogue derived from it, and a defensible coverage argument.
  • D
    V&V strategy and acceptance criteria. What each simulation, proving ground and field test actually proves — and what claim survives when it passes.
  • E
    Safety management and DIA. Safety plans, interface agreements with suppliers, tool qualification and confirmation measure planning.
  • F
    Assessment and approval readiness. Independent review before the assessor arrives, plus support through AFGBV and type approval submissions.
  • G
    Training. Practitioner-level courses on ISO 26262, SOTIF and safety for machine learning, taught against your own architecture rather than generic examples.
