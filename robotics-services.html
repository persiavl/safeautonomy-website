One hazard ecosystem

IEC 61508, the emerging AI-safety family and UL's certification standards are not three competing rulebooks. They are three views of the same question — can this robot hurt someone, and how do we know? Here is what each one covers, where the boundaries sit, and why the boundaries are the dangerous part.

Start here

Each standard family answers a different question

The fastest way to know which framework applies is to ask what has to be true for a person to be harmed. The three answers below are mutually exclusive as causes — and jointly exhaustive as an argument.

IEC 61508

"Does it fail dangerously?"

Something in the electrical, electronic or programmable electronic safety-related system breaks or was built wrong — a random hardware fault, a systematic design fault, a software defect — and the resulting malfunction leads to a hazard.

The system is faulted. Remove the fault and the hazard disappears.

TR 5469 · TS 22440 · TS 25223

"Can the AI be trusted?"

Nothing is broken. Every actuator responds correctly. The AI-enabled function is simply not reliable enough for the situation it meets, or it cannot say how confident it is when it should.

The system is fault-free. You cannot debug your way out; you have to improve the model, its data, or the way its uncertainty is used downstream.

UL 4600 · UL 3115

"Can you prove it?"

The robot may genuinely be safe. But safety that lives in engineers' heads and passing test logs is not a certifiable argument — and a customer, insurer or certification body cannot act on a claim it cannot audit.

The system may be safe and fault-free, but the evidence is not organised into a case anyone outside the engineering team can evaluate.

Why it matters

Why these standards had to exist

Each one was written because the previous one had run out of road. The sequence tells you exactly what each framework is blind to.

2010

IEC 61508 second edition — the generic functional safety baseline

2024

ISO/IEC TR 5469 published — AI functional safety guidance

2025

UL 3115 published — certification for AI-based products

IEC 61508 assumed deterministic software

Functional safety rests on a quiet assumption: that the correct behaviour of the system is fully specified, and the job of the safety lifecycle is to make the electronics deliver it reliably. For a safety-rated e-stop circuit or a fixed collision-avoidance interlock, that assumption holds — the logic is short, the inputs are bounded, and "correct" is unambiguous and testable line by line.

A robot that has to decide whether a shape on the floor is a pallet or a person breaks the assumption in exactly the way an automated vehicle does. The hard part is no longer delivering specified behaviour — it is knowing what the specified behaviour should be when the input space is effectively unbounded. IEC 61508 has no clause for a function that was implemented perfectly and is still wrong.

The AI-safety family named the gap — guidance, then specification

ISO/IEC TR 5469, published in 2024, was the first document to describe — at guidance level — the properties of AI systems that matter for functional safety, the risks they introduce, and candidate methods to treat them. It set the vocabulary rather than mandatory requirements.

ISO/IEC TS 22440 is the normative follow-on: a multi-part technical specification building directly on TR 5469, defining safety requirements for AI-enabled systems that adapt over time and how to integrate AI verification into the functional-safety lifecycle. Several parts are still in development at the time of writing — this is a standards family being written in real time, and we track it as it moves rather than waiting for a finished edition to start the conversation.

ISO/IEC TS 25223 narrows in on the single hardest sub-problem: how does a system know when it doesn't know? Uncertainty quantification is what separates a perception stack that fails loudly from one that fails silently and confidently.

The practical consequence. Under IEC 61508 you reduce risk with diagnostics and redundancy. Under the AI-safety family you reduce risk by improving the model, calibrating its confidence, or limiting what it is allowed to decide unsupervised. These are different engineering programmes with different budgets and different people in the room.

UL 4600 and UL 3115 addressed how you prove it

UL 4600 was written deliberately technology-agnostic: a safety case built from claims, arguments and evidence that applies as well to a delivery robot or an AMR fleet as it does to a self-driving car. It does not tell you how to build the robot — it tells you how to show that what you built is safe enough, and what is still open.

UL 3115, published in October 2025, is narrower and newer: an outline for the safety of AI-based products specifically, covering both AI-embedded products — autonomous robots among them — and pure digital AI products. It complements hardware and electrical safety rules rather than replacing them, and maps its criteria onto ISO, IEC and NIST frameworks so evidence built for one recognises the other.

And then the regulator or the customer arrives

None of this is optional once you want to sell or deploy. The EU's Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230, applicable from January 2027, replaces the old Machinery Directive with an explicitly widened scope that names autonomous mobile machinery, AI-based safety systems, industrial robots and cobots. ISO 3691-4 and ANSI/RIA R15.08 set the acceptance bar for AGVs and industrial mobile robots in Europe and the US respectively.

None of these bodies receive three standards. They receive one argument. If your evidence is organised by standard rather than by hazard, you will spend the weeks before a customer audit or a certification submission re-organising it under pressure.

The ecosystem

How they combine into one hazard ecosystem

There is only ever one hazard list. The standards do not partition the robot — they partition the causes. That single idea is what makes an integrated safety argument possible.

Step 1  ·  One hazard and risk analysis System definition and task envelope → hazardous events → safety functions. Performed once, jointly, not three times in three teams.
Branch A · IEC 61508

Fault-driven causes

Each safety function gets a SIL. Diagnostics, redundancy and architectural measures reduce the probability that a fault produces the hazard.

Branch B · AI safety family

AI-driven causes

The same safety functions are re-examined assuming zero faults. Model evaluation, calibrated uncertainty and monitoring bound the AI-related risk.

Branch C · UL 4600 · UL 3115

Assurance-case causes

Where branches A and B produce evidence, this branch supplies the structure that turns evidence into a claim a certification body can evaluate.

Step 2  ·  One integrated safety case Every safety function carries all three branches of evidence, with an explicit statement of what remains unproven.
Step 3  ·  Certification and deployment EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 conformity · ISO 3691-4 / ANSI RIA R15.08 acceptance · UL 4600 evaluation · UL 3115 certification · field monitoring feeding back into step 1.

The seams

Three places where real programmes leak

In our experience the findings that hurt most are never inside one standard. They sit exactly on the boundary, where each team assumes the other one owns it.

Seam 01

IEC 61508  ↔  AI safety

A safety function is verified to SIL 2 against a requirement that quietly assumed the AI's classification would be reliable. Functional safety confirms the implementation; nobody confirms the AI's actual behaviour under that assumption.

Fix: derive AI safety requirements from the same hazard analysis and safety functions, in the same requirement baseline — not a parallel document set.

Seam 02

AI safety  ↔  UL 4600 / 3115

A model's evaluation report sits in a data-science folder while the safety case simply states "the AI was tested" — without saying what, how much, or against which acceptance criterion.

Fix: treat every model evaluation result as first-class safety-case evidence, traced explicitly to the claim it supports.

Seam 03

UL 4600 / 3115  ↔  IEC 61508

The safety case cites a hardware fault-tolerance figure that was never actually verified at the SIL claimed — the case looks complete on paper while the underlying functional safety verification is still open.

Fix: never close a safety-case item against evidence that hasn't itself been independently verified.

Cross-cutting

Work that spans all three

The integration itself is a deliverable. These engagements are what turn three conformant workstreams into one argument.

Scope an engagement
  • A
    Integrated safety case architecture. A claim-and-evidence argument spanning IEC 61508, AI safety and UL evidence, with explicit context and residual risk statements.
  • B
    Joint hazard & AI-risk workshops. One hazard list, three causal branches, facilitated with your controls, perception and integration teams in the room together.
  • C
    Task envelope and scenario strategy. A structured operating envelope for the robot's environment, a scenario or test-condition catalogue derived from it, and a defensible coverage argument.
  • D
    V&V strategy and acceptance criteria. What each fault-injection test, model evaluation and safety-case review actually proves — and what claim survives when it passes.
  • E
    Safety management. Safety plans, interface agreements with suppliers and integrators, tool qualification and diagnostic-coverage planning.
  • F
    Certification readiness. Independent review before a UL 4600 evaluation, a UL 3115 certification, or an EU Machinery Regulation conformity assessment.
  • G
    Training. Practitioner-level courses on IEC 61508, the AI-safety family and UL 4600/3115, taught against your own architecture rather than generic examples.
